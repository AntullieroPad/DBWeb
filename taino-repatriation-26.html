Where things stand

What has changed since August

Two of the five constraints below have moved. Yale has answered the questions it can answer on its own, which shifts the remaining work to the community side — where it was always going to sit.

Holdings confirmed
Yale has confirmed that the Peabody holds Taíno-affiliated ancestral human remains, and that the 1930s expeditions covered Puerto Rico, Vieques, and Mona. The remains identified so far are fragmentary.
Yale's posture
The museum has indicated it can pursue a voluntary return outside NAGPRA through its own process, and that it can consider a return once an appropriate request is brought forward. Yale has described consultation as potentially including travel support, examination of the collection, community consultation, and transport. No return has been agreed to; what exists is a stated willingness to receive a request.
The representative question
The working goal is no longer a single authorized representative. It is a narrow consultation and repatriation body assembled for this purpose — drawing on island-based leadership, diaspora organizations, ceremonial figures, scholars, and people with museum and repatriation experience. Legitimacy here comes from relationships and process, not from a signature.
In motion
Conversations are underway with leadership across several Taíno organizations in Puerto Rico and the diaspora, with introductions being passed between them and in-person meetings this month. This is now a network rather than a set of separate approaches.
Despierta Boricua's role
Research, connecting people, and using our access to Yale. We are not the community of descent, and we are not the authority. Nothing about the body's composition or the question of disposition is ours to settle.
01 — The terrain

Five constraints, not one

Organizing conversations tend to collapse into the legal question, because it is the one with a familiar villain. But the legal door is closed by statute and will not open soon. The questions that actually decide this — who speaks, where ancestors go, on whose terms they are received — belong to us rather than to Congress.

Legal and jurisdictional

NAGPRA's reach is geographic. For the statute's purposes, "United States" means the 50 states and the District of Columbia; Puerto Rico falls outside that definition. Recognition alone does not cure the exclusion — the Guainía Taíno Tribe's recognition did not confer NAGPRA access. Changing this requires Congress. Rulemaking cannot reach what the statute does not authorize.

Closed by statute

Representative authority

Yale needs evidence that the party negotiating a return has been authorized by relevant Taíno communities. This does not require every Taíno organization to merge, or to agree on every question of identity or membership. It requires a legitimate process for deciding who speaks for this repatriation. A consultation and repatriation body convened for this purpose is now taking shape; its composition and mandate are being worked out by the organizations joining it.

In formation

Post-return conditions

Some institutions condition returns on the existence of a curatorial facility. Whether Yale would require this in a Taíno return has not been established; its published policy prioritizes preservation but allows communities to request alternate arrangements, subject to review. Any disposition standard should be negotiated with the descendant community rather than assumed in advance.

Unsettled

Disposition

Reburial, custodial holding, community stewardship, or a changed relationship with Yale. This is contested within Taíno communities themselves, and it is not an institution's question to settle.

Community's call

Transfer and ceremony

How ancestors are moved, who accompanies them, under what protocol, and who is present when they arrive. Almost entirely undiscussed, and the part most likely to be decided badly by default.

Unexamined
02 — Legal pathways

Three roads, and only one is open

One is a decade-long federal campaign. One is a legal theory no one has tested. The third is Yale's own voluntary process — the only road actually open, and the one that requires no federal recognition, because recognition would not help.

Pathway A

Amend the statute

Extend NAGPRA's reach to the Indigenous peoples of U.S. territories. Fixes the gap for everyone, permanently.

  • Congress and committees
  • National tribal coalitions
  • Legislation only — not rulemaking
  • 5–15 years · low odds
Pathway B

Litigation

Sue institutions for the theft of bodies and cultural heritage. Counsel at the Association on American Indian Affairs notes this has never been attempted.

  • Untested legal theory
  • Counsel, standing, a claimant body
  • Changes leverage even unfiled
  • No precedent · unknown odds
Pathway C · open

Negotiated institutional return

The Peabody's own repatriation policy, governed by an internal advisory panel. Available today, and where every realistic version of this ends.

  • Human Remains Advisory Panel
  • Division curators
  • A consultation body with a mandate
  • Yale has said it will receive a request
  • State Dept. recognition may assist
  • 1–3 years · viable now
Recognition is beside the point. Federal acknowledgment does not deliver NAGPRA rights to a people whose homeland the statute excludes — the Guainía Taíno Tribe's recognition did not. Recognition campaigns and repatriation are separate projects, and Pathway C waits on neither. What it does wait on is a body that can speak. Once one exists, the State Department may recognize it for repatriation purposes with U.S. and international institutions — which is the step that makes an institution less afraid of returning to the wrong party.
03 — The contested center

Where should they go?

This is the fork everything else hangs from, and the one an institution will quietly answer for you if no one answers it first. The "facility" line below records what museums have typically expected — not a standard any community owes them. Reburial is listed first because it is the option institutions are least likely to raise on their own.

Read the last one carefully. Co-stewardship asks the least of everyone and costs an institution almost nothing, which is precisely why it is the option most likely to be offered. That does not make it wrong. It does mean it should be chosen, not defaulted into.
04 — Process

What the Peabody actually requires

Steps one and two are ours, and step one is underway. Everything below them is Yale's routine machinery, and the museum has said it will engage once a request arrives.

Convene the consultation body

A body with a mandate to speak for this repatriation, assembled across island and diaspora leadership. In formation.

Community

Name the receiving entity and purpose

A specific destination and a stated purpose. Yale will ask what conditions ancestors will be held in; answering that is not the same as accepting the museum's standard as the test.

Community

Submit the formal request

To the Human Remains Advisory Panel, at ypm.hrap@yale.edu. Making the request opens the process.

HRAP

Consultation

The Division of Anthropology and the Repatriation Office engage the requesting party.

Anthropology

Recommendation and decision

HRAP advises. Final authority rests with the division curators.

Curators

Legal review

The Office of General Counsel reviews documentation after internal approval. It is not a gatekeeper.

Transfer

05 — Where you come in

What an allied organization can actually do

The current blocker is not a formal refusal from Yale. It is the absence of a Yale-specific representative mandate and an agreed path for disposition. An allied organization can help create the conditions for Taíno communities to resolve those questions; it cannot resolve them itself.

Convening

Help bring Taíno community leadership, scholars, and organizations into one room to work out what an authorized body looks like and who sits on it.

Research

Provenance depth on the 1934–38 expeditions, the separate Yale Biological Anthropology legacy collection, and precedent for returns where no curational facility was required.

Public record

Statements, campus press, and community media — held in reserve, and most useful if the institutional process stalls rather than before it starts.
