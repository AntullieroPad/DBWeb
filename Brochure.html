So what do you do with all this?
Before you book
  • Choose locally owned lodging; avoid AirBnBs in residential neighborhoods.
  • Learn the basics: PROMESA, La Junta, Vieques, Act 20/22, beach access.
While you're there
  • Eat at panaderías, kioskos, cafeterías, chinchorros, fondas, lechoneras, and locally owned restaurants.
  • Spend money outside the tourist corridor.
  • Learn one thing about where you are each day.
  • Tip well. Cheap to you does not mean cheap to live with.
  • Do not turn poverty, housing, or protest into content.
  • Hospitality is not consent to consume.
After you leave
  • Come back — differently.
Field Notes for the Yale Tourist
Driving: Narrow roads, potholes, inconsistent signage. Creative parking and sudden road rage are part of the road ecology. Google Maps does not understand the emotional life of a mountain road.
La Perla is a real community. Be careful. Do not wander in drunk, do not film people, and do not treat it like a dare.
Ask yourself: Would you take this picture if the person in it were rich, white, and able to complain to your dean? If not, put the phone away.

Puerto Rico is extraordinary — its people, its food, its music, its history, its resilience. It deserves to be experienced, celebrated, and loved.

Enjoyment does not require ignorance. But ignorance makes enjoyment cheaper.

Congratulations on your trip

You've chosen one of the Caribbean's most beautiful, vibrant, and — let's be honest — affordable destinations. Have you ever wondered why it's so affordable?

Puerto Rico is not "underdeveloped." It is over-managed. It is not "chaotic." It is over-governed from elsewhere. It is not "full of potential." It has been systematically redirected so that its potential accrues elsewhere.

But don't let that dampen your mood. The beaches are stunning.

Jíbaro's Translation Module
Hola / Buenos días — Not "local flavor." A basic acknowledgment that people exist before they serve you.
Gracias — The minimum. Not a substitute for tipping, patience, or respect.
Perdón / Con permiso — Useful when entering someone else's space, which you are.
Boricua — A Puerto Rican. Not just a cute synonym. A word tied to land, history, and belonging.
Janguear — To hang out. One of Puerto Rico's great contributions to the Spanish language.
Brutal — Good, intense, impressive. Not necessarily violent.
Chacho / Acho — A compressed emotional technology. Can mean surprise, frustration, affection, exhaustion, or all of the above.
Guagua — Bus. Also a reminder that public life exists beyond rental cars and Ubers.
Colmado — Corner store, social node, neighborhood infrastructure.
Caserío — Public housing community. Not a backdrop. Not a danger aesthetic. A real community with real people.
Gringo — Not always an insult.
In the spirit of Tavín Pumarejo: if the sentence sounds funny, listen harder. The joke usually knows more than the tourist.

The Beach™ — Rated

Puerto Rico's beaches are constitutionally public. Every single one. The law says no one can own them.

At some point you will notice something unsettling: the beach is pristine, the cafés are minimalist, the neighborhood feels curated.

Did you know?
Puerto Rico's constitution guarantees public access to all beaches. Despite this, luxury hotels and private developments have systematically restricted coastline access for decades. The view from your hotel balcony used to belong to everyone.
Puerto Rico is an archipelago

Puerto Rico is not one island. Vieques and Culebra are reachable by ferry for a few dollars. Both house bioluminescent bays — Mosquito Bay in Vieques is considered the brightest on the planet.

The U.S. Navy used Vieques as a bombing and weapons testing range for over 60 years. They displaced residents, contaminated the land and water, and left behind unexploded ordnance and elevated cancer rates that persist today.

Where to Stay™
The AirBnB question

Old San Juan is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of the oldest European-built cities in the Americas. It is also one of the most rapidly gentrifying neighborhoods in the Caribbean.

Between 2010 and 2020, Old San Juan lost a significant portion of its residential population as long-term rentals were converted to short-term tourist accommodations. The colorful buildings you'll photograph for Instagram were once — and in many cases very recently — someone's home.

We're not saying don't stay there. We're saying: know what you're staying in.

The Act 20/22 question

In 2012, Puerto Rico passed Act 20 and Act 22 — tax incentive laws designed to attract wealthy U.S. mainland investors by offering them near-zero tax rates on capital gains and passive income.

The result: a wave of wealthy transplants — many of them tech investors, crypto entrepreneurs, and hedge fund managers — relocated to Puerto Rico not out of love for the island but out of love for their tax bills. Local Puerto Ricans, who do not qualify for these incentives, have watched housing prices spike and their neighborhoods transform around them.

Some of these transplants also went to schools like Yale. Small world.

The Yale Connection

Yale trains people to analyze systems, extract insight, and move on. Fluent in critique. Allergic to implication. Yale's endowment has historically held investments in Puerto Rican debt instruments through hedge fund managers who sued the Puerto Rican government to ensure repayment.

Who Owns Paradise™?

In 2016, the U.S. Congress passed PROMESA — creating an unelected fiscal oversight board with the power to override decisions made by Puerto Rico's democratically elected government.

La Junta's mandate: ensure Puerto Rico pays its creditors. What it has overseen: closure of hundreds of public schools, cuts to pensions, reductions in healthcare funding, and the restructuring of $70 billion in debt. The median household income in Puerto Rico is ~$22,000. La Junta's job is not to change that number. Its job is to make sure creditors get paid first.

"PROMESA es pobreza."

And then there is LUMA — a private consortium contracted to operate Puerto Rico's electrical grid. Since taking over in 2021, Puerto Ricans have experienced some of the most prolonged and frequent blackouts in the island's history. Rates went up. The lights still go out. The contract stayed.

Did you know?
Puerto Rico's debt crisis is not the result of overspending. It is the result of decades of U.S. federal policy that prohibited Puerto Rico from filing for bankruptcy and allowed Wall Street to sell it predatory bonds. The island didn't borrow its way into crisis. It was structured into one.
