Yale College · Proposal to the Committee on Majors · December 2026

Archipelagos, Seas, and Diasporas

A Certificate in Caribbean Studies — and the resumption of a proposal Yale received in 1973.

The first formal undergraduate program at Yale centered on the Caribbean as a region in its own right. Not a new field. A recognition that the field is already here, taught across eight departments, and has never been allowed to say its own name.

Fifty-three years of asking Four waves · one unfinished proposal
01 — The architecture

Four axes, not four buckets

The certificate is organized around how Caribbean knowledge actually moves — across empires, languages, and oceans — rather than around the departments that happen to house it. Students take five to six credits, drawing from at least three axes.

Axis I

Mapping the Caribbean

Histories · Borders · Empires

The basin as a made thing. Colonial partition, the Lesser Antilles and Dutch Caribbean alongside the Greater, and the borders that empire drew and the region never agreed to.

Axis II

Caribbean Seeing & Saying

Aesthetics · Performance · Expression

Literature, music, theatre, and visual practice as theory rather than illustration — Caribbean thought in the forms it actually chose for itself.

Axis III

Becoming Caribbean

Sovereignty · Migration · Futurity

Status, statelessness, and self-determination; the plural diasporas of New Haven, New York, London, Amsterdam, and Montreal; and what the region is building next.

Axis IV

Knowing the Caribbean

Language · Archives · Digital Praxis

Kreyòl, Papiamentu, and Caribbean Spanish; the politics of who holds the record; and minimal-computing approaches to archives that were never built for us.

02 — The precedent

This has been asked before

Recovered from Yale's own archives: a complete Puerto Rican Studies Department proposal, and the correspondence showing exactly how the institution declined it. This is not context. It is the argument.

April 1972
Wave One

The demands

Despierta Boricua protests at the Yale Club of New York and issues an open letter of nine demands. PROP follows; so does La Casa — the first Puerto Rican cultural center in the Ivy League.

April–May 1973
Wave Two

The letters to Brewster

CENTRO's founding director Frank Bonilla writes to President Brewster offering the Center's help. Dean Luis Nieves adds his voice, citing programs at Brooklyn College, Hunter, Fordham, and Livingston. A White & Case attorney writes on behalf of three students, Manuel del Valle '74 Law their spokesman.

1973–74
Wave Two

The Jordan proposal

Howard Jordan and Boricuas Unidos submit a full departmental proposal — twelve terms of coursework, field research in Puerto Rico and urban communities, a speakers series, a senior essay. It names four failure modes at peer programs the certificate is now designed to avoid.

1973 →
The response

Seek foundation funding

Yale suggested the students approach foundations. The foundations were never approached. The department was never created. Del Valle would teach Yale's first Puerto Rican Studies seminars thirteen years later, in 1986.

2026
Now

The resumption

A certificate does not ask Yale to build something new. It asks Yale to formally acknowledge that this knowledge is already taught here — and that it counts.

“The Center has assured the student group at Yale that we stand ready to help in any way we can.”
Frank Bonilla, Director, CENTRO — to President Kingman Brewster, April 1973

06 — The horizon

The certificate is the proof of concept

It is deliberately the smallest viable ask. It is also the only structure that can generate the enrollment record a larger one will eventually require.

2027–28 Certificate

Five to six credits, two co-directors, an advisory board. Yale formally names the Caribbean as a field it teaches.

2030–32 Concentration

A track within an existing major, or a standalone concentration — built on the certificate's enrollment record.

2035 → Major

What was asked for in 1973, on a timeline the institution can no longer call premature.
